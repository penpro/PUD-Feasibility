    What was Prop 1?

    Kitsap Public Utility District (KPUD) already runs water, wastewater and fiber internet. Prop 1 asked voters whether KPUD may buy or build an electric system. A "yes" would not have changed anyone's power company or rates by itself. KPUD said it would first do a feasibility study, estimated at $300,000–$500,000.

    Who powers Kitsap today?

    Four utilities sell electricity in Kitsap County. A Kitsap PUD takeover would involve only one of them.

    Puget Sound Energy The takeover question

    A private, investor-owned company. Serves most Kitsap homes and businesses, about customers by PSE's count.

    Bonneville Power Administration

    The federal power agency. According to BPA, it sells directly to the Navy bases at Bremerton and Bangor. Those would not be part of a takeover.

    Peninsula Light Co.

    A member-owned co-op. Serves a small part of Kitsap. Exactly how many Kitsap customers is one of our open questions.

    Mason County PUD 3

    A public utility district. Also serves a small part of Kitsap. Customer count is an open question too.

    Why the 3.2 billion figure is wrong for Kitsap

    It comes from a website that takes Washington's average use per person ( MWh) and multiplies it by Kitsap's population. It shows the same per-person number for every county we checked. Washington's average includes data centers, paper mills and farm irrigation, which Kitsap doesn't have. The result is about higher than our estimate for PSE's Kitsap customers.

    You may also have heard "Kitsap needs around megawatts." That fits the highest-demand hour (our range: – MW), not average use (about MW).

    Quick units: 1 kWh is what your bill counts. 1 megawatt (MW) = 1,000 kW of power at one moment. 1 billion kWh = 1 terawatt-hour (TWh).

    The arguments, and how we'll test them

    Both sides have made claims that can be checked with numbers. Each one gets a direct test here. None is waved away.

    Concerns raised by PSE
    "KPUD has no public plan to pay for, run or power a utility for 130,000+ customers."
    Test: this project is building those numbers in public, one part at a time.
    "A new PUD would lose access to cheap federal hydropower and start with none."
    First result: partly right. A new PUD would start with no allocation, but BPA's policy lets a new public utility add up to average megawatts per rate period, with at most in total, shared by all new utilities. BPA still has to say whether KPUD would qualify (Part 2; what's still unknown).
    "Buying PSE's system would be very expensive. In Jefferson County, supporters estimated $47 million and it cost $109 million."
    Test: value Kitsap's equipment from PSE's own regulatory filings, and get Jefferson's original documents (Part 3; C4).
    "KPUD has never run an electric utility, let alone one this size."
    Test: staffing and cost benchmarks from utilities of similar size (Part 5; D4).
    "PSE has 70+ employees and 50+ contractors in Kitsap and can bring crews from across the West after storms."
    Test: crew sizing, mutual-aid agreements and federal outage records for PSE vs. public utilities (Part 5).
    "Leaving PSE would not lower prices."
    Test: side-by-side bills at the same usage, including loan payments and power-price risk (Part 6).
    Points raised by supporters
    "PSE rates are rising fast." PSE has asked for residential increases of about 30% over three years, as reported by Axios and KUOW.
    Test: the bill comparison uses PSE's filed and approved increases from the state regulator (Part 6).
    "Public utilities get first call on cheap federal hydropower." BPA must give preference to public utilities.
    First result: partly right, within limits. If KPUD qualified, BPA's limits would cover about of Kitsap's average use at first and at most about later. More than that from BPA would be at its higher-priced Tier 2 rate (Part 2; what's still unknown).
    "A PUD is run by locally elected commissioners, in public meetings, under public-records law."
    Test: that's not a dollar question. We note it, and voters weigh it.

    Sources: PSE press release (July 28, 2026); KUOW (Oct 30, 2026); Axios (Oct 29, 2026); KPUD Fact Publication (Aug 2026). Know a claim we missed, from either side? Send it (A2).

    Questions

    Who made this? Is it for or against public power?

    Wesley Weaver started it: a decade in nuclear power generation, five years in data centers, and ten years running a technology consulting business doing engineering work and cost-benefit studies, now paired with a computer science degree for the data modeling. It's independent and open source, not affiliated with Kitsap PUD, PSE or any campaign.

    The study isn't for or against public power. It answers "what would it cost, and what would bills look like," and tests claims from both sides the same way. You don't have to trust the author: every number is sourced, so you can check it yourself.

    Prop 1 looks like it's failing. Why keep going?

    If "No" holds, KPUD says its own study won't move forward. That order was KPUD's choice: it decided to ask voters first because of the study's cost and past votes elsewhere. State law doesn't appear to require a vote just to study the idea, and a new vote on authority couldn't happen before the 2028 general election. The underlying questions don't go away: PSE's rate increases, future proposals, and other communities weighing the same choice. Public, checkable numbers help whatever comes next.

    Can I trust these numbers?

    Don't take our word for it. Every number is labeled, sourced and reproducible from public files, and the code that produces it is public. If you find a mistake, report it (E3). Corrections are part of the project.

    What's a kWh, a megawatt, a terawatt-hour?

    A kWh (kilowatt-hour) is the unit on your bill. A megawatt (MW) is 1,000 kilowatts of power at one moment; utilities size wires and power contracts in MW. A terawatt-hour (TWh) is a billion kWh, the scale of a whole county's yearly use.

    Why does peak demand matter so much?

    Power lines, substations and some power contracts have to handle the single highest-demand hour of the year, usually a cold winter evening. Average use tells you how much energy to buy; the peak tells you how big the system has to be. We don't have Kitsap's real peak yet (help wanted C1).

    Where does " billion kWh" come from?

    Two independent methods. One works backward from the county's own 2022 climate inventory, which was built on utility data. The other multiplies PSE's customer count by how much homes and businesses use at similar nearby utilities. They land within about 10% of each other. A public records request for the county's underlying data would replace the estimate with a measurement.
